1. Project Context, Development Objectives and Design

1.1 Context at Appraisal

Country and Sector Background

Paraguay pursued the twin goals of addressing economic stagnation and of fighting
corruption. The country’s economic program was particularly oriented towards
supporting agro-industry and diversification of exports. Addressing governance concerns
was a means to help improve both the performance and perception of public institutions.
Both sets of goals were seen to require enhancement of human capital, and hence allied
plans to increase investment in education and health.

Improved infrastructure was seen as critical for the success of the government’s
programs. Shortcomings in both the quality and coverage of infrastructure adversely
affected the competitiveness of Paraguay’s producers, as quality and coverage were seen
to be lagging behind regional averages. Specific challenges arose from Paraguay being a
land locked country, enhancing the requirement to identify and exploit opportunities to
reduce transport and logistics costs. Additionally, better infrastructure, especially in the
case of rural roads, were seen as a necessary input to poverty alleviation, through
improving access to basic services and business opportunities.

Increased and better focused expenditure on roads was needed to secure quality and
coverage improvement. In the road sector more resources were required not only to
secure an improvement in the national paved and non-paved networks, but also to sustain
maintenance of these networks. At the time of appraisal, the proportion of resources
devoted to routine and periodic maintenance was assessed to be far below what was
required. The situation was aggravated by a failure of a recently created (and later
closed) road fund to provide any incremental resources to maintenance over and above
the national budget. Additionally, better focused expenditures needed to be allied with
specific measures to improve planning, efficiency and results orientation in the public
road agencies as well as to secure greater transparency in decision making.

Road sector policies and institutions were seen as a constraint to improved sector
performance. At the time of appraisal, the Government determined a need to improve
the ministry responsible for road management, the Ministerio de Obras Publicas y
Comunicaciones (MOPC). The ministry controlled 10% of the national investment
budget and employed 2% of the public sector payroll. MOPC however lacked a clear
sector policy oriented primarily towards maintenance, suffered from significant
institutional weaknesses, especially those related to planning and operated in an
inefficient manner due to gaps in human capacity and poorly adopted internal procedures.




                                             1
In common with many public sector institutions, MOPC lacked transparency in decision
making and suffered from a poor perception form road users and the public in general.


Rationale for Bank Involvement

Road sector investment was seen as highly relevant to the CAS approved in November
20033. The CAS was designed to support financial and fiscal reforms; to help improve
governance and transparency in public institutions; to promote sustained growth,
especially in rural areas; and to enhance social inclusion in order for Paraguay to meet its
Millennium Development Goals (MDG). The proposed lending operation in the road
sector was seen to be relevant to both the growth and inclusion dimensions of the CAS.
Additionally the operation was expected to facilitate pursuing a transparency agenda
specific to the needs of the sector.

The Bank possessed relevant country and sector expertise to help Paraguay improve
road sector performance. The Bank had been involved in the road sector in Paraguay
over a long period of time, with the most recent operation being the Eighth Highway
Project (IBRD Loan 3685-PA). Accordingly, the Bank had a lot of knowledge of the
institutional shortcomings of the sector, in particular regard to the planning and carrying
out of maintenance, so was well placed to advise on new directions that could be taken to
address the issues. The Bank also had by this time developed a lot of experience,
globally but more specifically in the Latin America region in countries such as Uruguay
and Argentina in the ways to improve road management. This included the increased use
of private sector in road maintenance through performance base contracting – an area in
which the Government had expressed interest.

Lessons Learned from Past Operations

Present and future focus for support to the sector should be on maintenance. A critical
change with respect to past sector operations and priorities was an enhanced focus on
maintenance and hence on improving the sustainability of sector investment. The design
of the project was to recognize and address specific challenges as follows: establishing
planning and expenditure priority for maintenance; addressing weak sector
implementation and management; improving budget allocation and reducing the number
of competing projects in the sector, through clarifying priorities and targeting a greater
share of counterpart funding to maintenance.

Weak sector institutions can hamper implementation performance. With specific
reference to the experience of the Eighth Highway Project, insufficient attention given to
building and sustaining sector planning and execution capacity can be expected to have a
negative effect on sector management and ultimately on project implementation. Thus in
addition to strengthening MOPC in its various critical functions, specific actions were

1.4 Main Beneficiaries

The population of Paraguay as a whole was expected to benefit from the Project through
its positive effects on competitiveness, employment and economic growth. Road users in
general were expected to benefit from improved network quality and enhanced
maintenance to reduce vehicle operating costs and travel times, especially on the road
corridors to be improved under the Project. The direct beneficiary communities in the
departments of San Pedro, Caaguazu and Caazapa were expected to see their access to
opportunities improved through the secondary road development in their areas as well as
through the expansion of job opportunities in the roads and other sectors. Finally through
the development of Indigenous People’s Plans (PPI) for each department, the specific
cultural and economic needs of these groups were considered.

2. Key Factors Affecting Implementation and Outcomes

2.1 Project Preparation, Design and Quality at Entry

Project Preparation and Readiness for Implementation

The Project was strongly linked to national objectives and CAS strategy. The PDO
speaks to the contribution of the Project towards improving economic growth and
alleviating poverty through improved road management strategy. The Project appeared
to be well integrated into the framework of support provided by the Bank to Paraguay
under the CAS. The Project’s particular contributions to the country’s objectives were
through the promotion of sustained growth, especially in the rural areas, and to the
enhancement of social inclusion, and through improving the coverage and efficiency of
basic social services.

The Project was carefully defined over an extended preparatory phase. Three
preparation missions were carried out in 2004, as were a further two in 2005. The Bank
provided significant financial resources to the preparation phase, inter alia through the
proceeds of a PHRD grant. Throughout the preparation process, the broad outlines of
Project design were maintained. However one important element – that is the
government and transparency improvement plan (IGAP) – emerged quite late in the
preparation process. This factor was compensated by the additional attention given to
this relatively new subject area for MOPC, including workshops carried out in March
2016 ahead of Project appraisal.

                                                    11
Assessment of Design

The Project had a strong, upfront focus on establishing a sustainable road
management strategy. Primacy was given to the development of a Road Strategy to be
the principal underpinning of a more rational and well-resourced plan for the sector than
had been the case in the past. This can be considered important in that the emphasis on
the early development of the road sector strategy aligned with the priority accorded to it
in the PDO. Similarly, many of the key institutional and supporting legal steps were
identified during preparation and slated for early implementation

Project design was generally appropriate, but with some important provisos
concerning the results framework. The three broad components of the Project-
covering strengthening strategic planning and road management; improvement and
maintenance of the paved road network; and improvement and maintenance of the
unpaved road network - align well with the overarching objective of establishing a
sustainable road management strategy. The components match up well with the three
intermediate objectives under the PDO. The implied prioritization of these objectives is
appropriate, starting from capacity improvement through adoption of key improvements
affecting the main network through to complimentary attention being given to enhancing
accessibility.

The original results framework does not match well with the PDO and the intermediate
objectives (see Section 2.3). This did create difficulties to effectively track performance
against PDO, especially in the earlier years of the Project. A number of the original KPI,
such as reduction in vehicle operating costs, increase in public passenger transport, and
reduction in transportation times, are not specifically relevant to the PDO and the critical
expected outcomes of the Project. However in the case of the development of MOPC
capacity the KPI and intermediate indicators, such as the creation to the road planning
unit and the implementation of a road strategy, appear to be appropriate and measurable
during the life of the Project. There was also a mismatch between the wording of the
PDO in the LA and in the PAD resulting in the misallocation of two intermediate
outcome indicators as mentioned in Table 2 above. In addition the target kilometers for
the performance based maintenance contracts and the unpaved road improvements were
not clearly delineated as those to be funded through the Project - reference is made to
broader targets depending on actions and financing outside of the scope of the Project.

The Project’s risks were generally identified and correctly rated. The overall risk
rating for the Project was “High”. The following specific elements were identified: the
risk of varying Government support as ministers and key officials change; the risk
created by working through a sector institution with weak technical and human capacity,
and the risk associated with the lack of prior knowledge and experience in working with
private sector performance based maintenance contracts. Also mentioned were the
associated risks to Project implementation arising from limited knowledge in Bank
procurement capacity and from the potential inadequacies or delays in local funding. As
discussed below however, the strategies adopted by the Bank and the Borrower to
mitigate these risks during implementation were not always effective.



                                             12
The Project was likely to require significant enhancement to weak local capacity.
The creation of the UPGP as part of the design can be considered an important step
towards strengthening the core structures for project management. Beyond that however,
the Project required significant institutional strengthening, not only in planning but also
in more specialized areas such as performance contract (GMANs) management, micro-
enterprises, tolls management and governance and transparency. Project preparation
recognized the past difficulties encountered in assuring sufficient, stable financing for the
sector, but perhaps paid insufficient attention to developing the measures required to
resolve this. This inevitably had an adverse impact on local capacity to prepare and
launch implementation of the Project.


2.2 Implementation

Overview

Implementation presented significant challenges throughout the life of the Project.
The IBRD Loan was extended on several occasions in order to provide the space and
opportunity required to complete Project activities. The original closing date was end
June 2012, whereas the final completion date was end June 2016, largely due to later than
expected start- up of the civil works activities. There appear to be many contributing
factors to these delays, including recurrences in shortfalls in finance and personnel and
also the impact of changes in government administration, as discussed below. The
Project remained challenging to implement within the available resources and
experiences of MOPC, given these included new forms of works contracts such as
GMANs and micro-enterprises as well as an expanded social agenda, including
governance and transparency. A number of contracts experienced delays in both in terms
of procurement and implementation, due partly to limitations on knowledge and
experience of Bank policies and procedures, especially in the cases of procurement and
social assessment.

The Bank on balance underestimated these challenges, but nonetheless provided a
lot of support towards improving implementation. Many of the challenges and the
risk of delay associated with them could readily have been anticipated, as they were
included under the appraisal risk assessment. The Project introduced a number of
institutional changes associated with new initiatives in road planning and management.
The capacity to absorb such changes was limited by knowledge and experience in the
sector, exacerbated by the impact of changes in administration which occurred at end of
2008 and 2012 respectively.

The Bank however did field frequent supervision missions in the initial years of Project
implementation and provided a list of prescribed activities for regular, mostly at least
annual review, can be seen as providing a useful framework for getting Project




                                             13
implementation on track after the slow start17. The Project however had very low
disbursement due to delays in contracting activities through at least 2011, and it would be
have been appropriate to downgrade the ratings at an earlier stage to draw attention to
Bank management and the Government to the problems encountered.

The Bank was though effective in assisting in the development of an action plan in
September 2011 which helped improve Project performance through a specific focus on
action steps in four major areas: MOPC capacity improvement; acceleration of the
GMANS contracts; acceleration of the unpaved road improvements; and increasing IBRD
Loan disbursements. Implementation was generally more effective from this time on.
This however was not reflected in the ratings for implementation progress which were for
the most part “moderately unsatisfactory” up to Project closure. This rating appears to
reflect largely the problems encountered in completing the required social mitigation
measures, as discussed below. There were no problems of a comparable magnitude
otherwise.

Factors generally within Government Control

The Project suffered a long delay in loan effectiveness. The IBRD Loan was finally
declared effective in January 2008, whereas this had initially been targeted for January
2007. Three technical missions of the Bank were fielded between Board approval in July
2016 and the date of declaration of effectiveness. As there were no particular conditions
of effectiveness, these delays can be attributed fully to the length of time taken to secure
the necessary approvals and legal authority for the government of Paraguay to commit to
the Loan.

Early stages of implementation were adversely affected by financial and personnel
shortfalls. The start-up of Project activities was slower than anticipated in the
implementation plan established at appraisal. Not only in 2007, but also in 2008 and
2009 by which time the Project had become effective, the Government did not provide all
the budgetary resources required for the Project to be implemented. This can be seen as a
critical factor in two senses: firstly because all Project activities were co-funded to the
amount of 30% by counterpart resources; and secondly because these shortfalls
contributed to delays in the appointment of key MOPC staff and also consultants critical
to the implementation of various Project activities.




17

Changes in government administration had some adverse effects on progress of the
Project. The elections that took place towards the end of 2008 brought in a new
administration and ended 60 years of rule by the Colorado party. Thus the transition that
took place was more profound than any previous recent transition and more time was
required to effect changes in ministerial and key official appointments, including those in
MOPC. This transition happened at a time when the Project was just about to start
implementation. The transition at the end of 2012 also had a significant impact in that
further delays of 12-15 months occurred in launching the Project activities and associated
institutional strengthening that had been identified at that time.



Factors generally within Implementing Agency control

Procurement activities suffered early delays. Even taking into consideration the
delays in Project effectiveness, very little advance activity, especially in regard to
procurement had taken place by end 2007. 2008 saw little progress made up to August,
by which time election preparations had kicked in. Consequently, the implementing
agency was not in a position to launch any bidding for works until the middle of 2009.
These delays do appear to have been mostly tied to internal regulations – which factor
appears to have been steadily mitigated over the course of the Project – rather than due to
lack of knowledge or capacity. As a consequence, the GMANS contracts only got under
way in September 2009 (GMANS 5), March 2010 (GMANS 1) and in December 2010
(GMANS 0 and 2)18. In the same vein, the contracts for the improvement of unpaved
roads commenced only in May 2013 (San Pedro), June 2013 (Caazapa) and August 2013
(Caazuagu).

Changes in design contributed to higher than anticipated capital costs. The GMANs
contracts were let based on initial basic designs, with the responsibility of the winning
bidders to provide proposed final designs for MOPC approval. In this process, the
amount of rehabilitation works tended to increase and thus estimated capital costs were
higher than at appraisal. This situation seems to have arisen partly due to the fact that the
initial designs dated from before the Project was approved and from the fact that the rate
of deterioration of the pavement was greater than anticipated. A further contributory
factor was that MOPC might be favorable to an approach that would lead to an
improvement in the quality of the road over a maintenance-only intervention.

have provided very useful input for an assessment of road user satisfaction and
beneficiary impact – and for the mentioned KPIs, final target data was not planned to be
collected until after the conclusion of the Project. Therefore, this information was going
to be of little value in measuring performance during the life of the Project.

Some of the original PDO indicators were not relevant - other PDO and
intermediate indicators were missing . A number of issues occurred in the specification
of the indicators, arising effectively from the incoherence of the results framework with
the PDO. The original PDO indicators did not align very well with the PDO, with the
exception of the one related to the creation of the road strategy. For example, the
indicators relating to vehicle operating costs and, transportation times would have been
relevant to a road improvement, but much less to one oriented toward a sustainable
(maintenance based) road management strategy. None of the original PDO indicators
appear to directly address promotion of efficient planning and resource allocation or
improvement of access to excluded rural communities. In fairness however, there were a
number of useful intermediate indicators, some of which were later to be provide the
basis of revised PDO indicators under the January 2014 restructuring. Even after that,
there were still some noticeable gaps, specifically no indicator at either level to track
resource mobilization for road sector expenditures. Additionally, it would have been
helpful to have an indicator to more explicitly track achievement of transparency and
governance under the IGAP.

Implementation

The January 2014 restructuring addressed some, but not all, of these weaknesses.
The restructuring correctly recognized and exploited an opportunity to review the results
framework and generally came up with a number of improvements which were helpful in
the sense of sharpening performance monitoring in the latter years of the Project. The
resulting results framework aligned better with the PDO than did the original version19
Under the restructuring, of the original PDO indicators only one was (partially) retained;
four indicators were reassigned as intermediate indicators and one indicator was dropped
altogether. The scope of one indicator - increased level of road user satisfaction was
modified and its scope reduced in terms of geographical area. Four new indicators were

The UPGP appears to have been generally diligent and effective in preparing regular

2.4 Safeguard and Fiduciary Compliance

Social Assessment. The involuntary resettlement (OP 4.12) and indigenous peoples’ (OP
4.10) policies were triggered. Timely completion of all the required actions was an issue
during project implementation, despite efforts made to ensure general compliance with
the safeguards under all works contracts as well as to ensure effective communication
with all affected parties. Generally speaking, capacity to manage the main social impact
of road projects improved during the life of the Project. In specific regard to involuntary
resettlement, there were very few issues arising. One exception was that there were some
cases of illegal occupation of the right of way that had to be addressed. There was some
land take involved with the new toll stations (specifically at Ita and Yparacai) and with
the unpaved road component. With one of the unpaved road sections (San Pedro Lot 1),
there was a specific issue concerning the routing of the improved road. This generated
significant dissension among and between the served communities contributing to a delay
in the completion of the works. The implementation of the Indigenous People’s Plans
(PPI) suffered significant delays in the implementation of some sub-components,
specifically the construction of three indigenous training centers associated with each
department (San Pedro, Caaguazu and Cazaapa), these only being completed by mid-
2016. An issue for future sustainability will be that the funding for continued operation



                                            17
and maintenance of these centers falls outside of the purview of the road sector.
However a plan for the operational phase has been prepared and the roles of the Instituto
Nacional del Indigena (INDI) and the municipal councils among others have been spelled
out. It would likely have facilitated the PPI had the implementation arrangements
provided for specific contributions from the national and departmental agencies
responsible for indigenous people’s issues at an earlier stage. This may have helped, for
example, to avert delays associated with INDI’s initial disagreement on the plans on
where to locate and how to operate the centers.

Environmental Assessment. The environmental assessment (EA) safeguard (OP 4.01) was
triggered. No significant or unanticipated environmental impacts arose during works ’
implementation. Generally speaking, capacity to manage the main environmental impacts
of road projects was improved during the life of the Project. Delays in the issuance of
environmental licenses for works under the Project were a factor during implementation –
though there was some improvement in this regard over the course of time. A further
concern were the delays that occurred in certain cases in the issuance of the final
environmental reports (REFICAs) by MOPC, as this factored into the completion of
handover of works by the contractors.

Financial Management. The Project did at times - early on at least - suffer from delays in
the submission of reports and from shortcomings in the justification of eligible
expenditures. The most recent audit reports have however contained clean opinions on
the Project accounts, though drawing attention to the need to sharpen the analysis of non-
eligible expenditures for the Project. Bank supervision of the financial management
aspects of the Project was effective.

Procurement. The Project suffered some initial delays in contracting of works and
consulting services due to lengthy and balky internal procedures. In regard to specific
aspects of procurement, notably in regard to performance based contracts for which no
standard documents existed at the outset of the Project, there were some capacity issues
and lack of knowledge and experience of the relevant Bank policies and procedures.
These shortcomings were mitigated to a significant extent by training and orientation
provided by Bank specialized staff during implementation. Bank supervision of the
procurement management aspects of the Project was effective. A concern towards the
end of the Project was that there was no procurement specialist in place in MOPC, though
this appears to have been subsequently and satisfactorily addressed.

2.5 Post-completion Operation/Next Phase

A follow up Transport Connectivity Project (Loan No. 86380-PY, P147278) in the
amount of US$100 million was approved July 25, 2016. The operation builds on the
lessons learned from the Project, particularly in terms of the design and implementation
of performance based (rehabilitation and) maintenance contracts, and benefits from the
capacity built in MOPC in this area. The selection of roads for the new project derives
from the planning and strategic work carried out under the Project, especially under the
planning unit (DPV) which came into being as a result of the Project. Sustainability and



                                            18
benefits to be perceived by road users and the community at large will be enhanced by
the wider geographical scope of the new project and by a new area of focus on road
safety. The new project is expected to continue the process of deepening MOPC capacity
for planning and management, especially in regard to maintenance.

The Country Partnership Framework (CPF) for 2015-2018, Report No. 82487-PY has
been approved, with the specific goal of supporting the National Development Plan
(NDP) in regard to poverty reduction, especially in rural areas. The CPF pillars are
poverty reduction; inclusive economic growth; and international connectivity. The recent
growth record in Paraguay has been impressive, but inequality remains a challenge.
Improved road corridors remain vital to prospects for increased production and trade,
through improved logistics and reduced transport costs.


3. Assessment of Outcome

The evaluation of Project outcome is based on the version of the PDO in the LA. The
key objective statement to be evaluated is “to establish a sustainable road management
strategy” whose result would be the upgrading and maintenance of the national road
network as measured by three specific intermediate objectives as follows: strengthening
MOPC institutional capacity, promotion of efficient planning and adequate resource
allocation for rehabilitation and maintenance of the road network, and improvement of
access of excluded rural communities to the primary road network in the mentioned
departments. The intermediate objectives are considered to represent the three main
dimensions of the sustainable road management strategy and thus are the key objectives
evaluated to determine Project outcome.


3.1 Relevance of Objectives, Design and Implementation

The Relevance of the PDO is rated as High

The PDO was highly relevant to the development and sectoral challenges faced by
Paraguay and to the strategies adopted by the government to address them. The PDO
focused both on improvements to resource allocation, especially in regard to maintenance
of the road network at both national and departmental level, as well as on strengthening
sectoral strategy and institutional capacity to sustain road management. The expected
impacts in terms of improved logistics, reduced transport costs and reduced social
exclusion remain highly relevant to Paraguay’s present situation. The economy remains
small and open and heavily dependent on agricultural production and foreign trade. The
Project maintained a balance between economic impacts and social impacts, with the
latter including the contribution towards improved governance and transparency in a
sector supported by major public expenditure. The PDO is also consonant with the
recently approved National Development Plan (NDP) for the period 2014-2030, which
seeks to address sustainable economic growth and poverty reduction over the medium
term in Paraguay.



                                            19
The Relevance of Project design is rated as Substantial

The PDO to establish a sustainable road management strategy links well to the three
immediate objectives which are the means to bring about upgrading and maintenance of
the road network. The Project’s three components align well with the intermediate
objectives in terms of their overarching intent. The structure of the Project allowed for a
strong institutional strengthening component, much of which was successfully
implemented early on in the Project, allied to highway rehabilitation and maintenance
and improvement of access all of which are very relevant to the establishment of a
sustainable road management strategy. Project outputs contribute directly to the
achievement of the intermediate objectives. The allocation of funds under the Project to
the three main components is broadly appropriate, though some reallocation was required
in view of the higher costs incurred in carrying out the GMANs contracts which had a
higher rehabilitation component than originally anticipated. The results framework had
its shortcomings, especially at the level of the original PDO indicators which was
addressed to a large extent later in the Project life. The intermediate indicators were
broadly well designed (with perhaps a couple of omissions) and provided a basis for
assessing intermediate outcomes.


3.2 Achievement of Project Development Objectives


The outcome indicators for the PDO were changed significantly following a January
2014 restructuring, as discussed in Section 1.3. Achievement is assessed against the
targets for these revised indicators. Most of the original PDO indicators are not
considered to be well aligned to the PDO as discussed in Section 2.1 and 2.3 above. In
regard to the intermediate outcomes and associated targets, the evaluation also considers
what was retained following the January 2014 restructuring. There were fewer changes
than was the case for the PDO indicators, but in 3 cases an original target was changed
and the resultant impact is discussed below in the relevant section.

PDO – Establishment of a sustainable road management strategy Rating: Substantial

The Project sought to support the establishment of a road management strategy which
was to be a substantial improvement on what went before it in the sense of: an
overarching focus on maintenance and preservation of the road network; the development
and sustaining of knowledge and capacity in road sector planning; the introduction of
methods, policies and procedures to increase efficiency of operations and resource use;
and effectively integrating the various levels of the road network, with particular regard
to the rural communities enjoying limited access to social and economic opportunity.

In overall terms, the Project is evaluated as having substantial achievements in regard to
the PDO. The changes that have been supported through the Project – with its limited
available resources - provide a good foundation for further future improvements which
will enhance the quality of the road network and the effectiveness of all sources of
financing for the sector. This is commendable the more so bearing in mind the weak


                                            20
starting capacity of MOPC taken together with a political and administrative environment
that had not been historically supportive of the type of changes contemplated through the
Project.

As summarized below, all five indicators can be assessed as having been achieved.
Notably, the road strategy and five-year plan (2008-2012) provided a platform for further
plan updates (from 2013) and input to the National Development Plan (through 2032).
Budget execution – for which an indicator was added in 2014 – has shown an improving
trend. In the final year of the project was 89% of planned expenditure compared to a
target of 75% and to an outcome of 83% in 2015. This is indicative of greater realism in
estimating work output and better planning of the use of the resources made available.
These achievements are buttressed by overarching improvements in national budget
management that occurred during the life of the Project, such as clearer distinction
between capital and recurrent spending.

There is one proviso to this assessment. In the case of the percentage of users reporting

survey comfortably exceed the baseline and target values. However there is a “halo

3.3 Efficiency

Rating: Modest

Overall, returns to the use of funding provided through the Project are positive, though
lower than estimated at appraisal. The aggregate ex ante estimate economic rate of
return was 33% whereas the estimate ex post ERR is 21%


                                                     25
The main contributory factors have been: higher than estimated capital costs as many of
the road sections covered required more rehabilitation than had been expected to sustain
the targeted level of improvement in roughness; and delays in both the procurement and
the implementation of works which resulted in the postponement of benefits to be derived
from the roads improvements. However these factors have been partly offset by stronger
than forecast traffic growth, estimated at 7% per annum compared to 2.5% ex ante, which
have helped to drive higher quantified benefits.

 For the GMANS contracts, economic analysis was carried out using the HDM-IV model
which simulates life cycle conditions and costs. For the ex ante analysis, vehicle fleet
characteristics were defined and economic costs were estimated at 85% of financial costs.
The analysis was carried out over a 15 year period with an adopted discount rate of 12%.
Traffic was derived from data recorded on toll stations over the period 1995-2004. The
ex ante analysis considered three sections that were originally set for the financing by the
Project, for a total of 968 km.

For the ex-post analysis, the same methodology was used but considering the actual costs
of the works carried out as well as the estimated actual traffic growth. Only data for
GMANS 0 and GMANS 1 were calculated given that GMANS 2 works were not in the
end carried out under the Project. The actual capital costs for the rehabilitation and
periodic maintenance elements of the contract were identified, as were the actual
construction periods. The total length of roads improved was 489 km. Traffic was
estimated using 2011-2014 data for the same toll stations used in the ex ante analysis. In
the absence of new VOC numbers, the original estimates were used, but updated for
inflation.

3.5 Overarching Themes, Other Outcomes and Impacts

(a) Poverty Impacts, Gender Aspects, and Social Development

The Project sought to address social exclusion in rural areas through adopting a
participatory process for the selection of roads for improvement in the three selected
departments. This has been supported by the implementation of specific measures under
the PPI which have helped to increase inclusion and enhance responsiveness to
previously marginalized communities.

Civic participation in the planning and management of the road sector in Paraguay has
been more generally assisted by the implementation of the IGAP. The program has
created new monitoring tools and radically increased the public availability of
information on the road network. The design of the web site has enhanced accessibility
through the use of graphic interfaces. There is also provision for complaints and other
feedback to be provided to MOPC.

Action in regard to gender was an integral element of the program to promote micro-
enterprises for routine maintenance on the unpaved network. Female participation in
these micro-enterprises – now at 26% of total - was enhanced through encouraging



                                              28
participation and stressing the overarching social development goals to which income
generated from employment on routine maintenance could contribute.

(b) Institutional Change/Strengthening

Within the framework of a public sector ministry responsible for public works, the
Project was instrumental in promoting significant change and strengthening, based on the
adoption of a new road management strategy. This assistance was oriented first and
foremost to enhancing the basic planning and management functions, but also covered
innovation in contracting, strengthening of toll management and revenue generation as
well as the integration of a number of critical activities linked to enhanced governance
and transparency in the road sector. These changes were supported by the Government
during Project implementation although the achievement of some of the original targets
was delayed or incomplete, in particular regard to the road inventory.

The IGAP has proven to be a good instrument for introducing and deepening the
institutional commitment to greater transparency. The Paraguay experience is seen as a
model which has been shared very widely with other road sector institutions in the
region. Particular mention can be made of the very effective tool for monitoring
contracts that was developed. Starting from a quite basic model, the functionality of the
tool and the general accessibility of information was steadily improved over time.

Overall however it should be recognized that the Project played a leading and critical role
in the transformation of the maintenance culture in Paraguay through the innovation
introduced through performance based contracting. By the end of the Project more than
50% of the primary road network was under some form or other of multi-year works
contract, managed on the basis of achieving and maintaining a level of service to the road
user. Also all road sections that had been covered by GMANS contracts under the
Project continued to receive attention for maintenance - and rehabilitation where required
- after the closure of the Project.

4. Assessment of Risk to Development Outcome
Rating: Moderate

Concerning the infrastructure improvements secured through the Project, it will be
critical to sustain robust maintenance arrangements and the required funding to go
with it. The introduction through the Project of performance-based contracting has
helped to establish a stronger maintenance culture and organization which now appears to
apply generally to the network regardless of funding source. This is very evident for the


                                            29
national network, perhaps less so for the departmental network, though a good start has
been made there with the introduction of the micro-enterprises. At the macro level,
establishing and sustaining the right levels of funding may continue to be a challenge in
the absence of dedicated funding flows other than tolls. However improvements in
planning and management will certainly assist in using the resources available as
efficiently as possible.

Concerning institutional development, the Project made good inroads into a
substantial, ambitious agenda that had been set out at the outset. The effectiveness
of MOPC has clearly been enhanced with institutional capacity built in a number of
areas. The approval of the IBRD funded follow on Transport Connectivity Project will
help assure the provision of further support for institutional strengthening, with particular
regard to road management where some activities, such as road inventory, remain to be
completed. The implementation of the IGAP has helped in regard to the development of
new instruments to better monitor contracting and works performance as well as to
provide a conduit for better public information and civic participation. The accessibility
of information and the quality of its presentation has improved over the life of the
Project, providing a firm foundation for future initiatives in this area.

In regard to social development, a continued, strong relationship with INDI and the
local authorities will be needed. The Project was successful in developing a
comprehensive social management framework, including an IPP. Implementation of the
IPP was subject to significant delays, due in part to difficulties encountered in identifying
and carrying out mitigation measures in the three departments. There was clearly an
element of learning here for the road sector and MOPC in particular. But going forward,
the eventual achievements cannot be seen as an end in itself but a stepping stone to a
wider, more concerted effort to manage the social aspects of road maintenance. In this
regard relationships with INDI and the local authorities will have to be sustained and
improved.

6. Lessons Learned

   a. Performance based contracting can provide the critical underpinning for the
      strategy to improve and sustain road maintenance. A pivotal feature of the
      Project was the introduction of PBC, using long term maintenance contracts for
      the first time in Paraguay. This can be judged as successful in terms of making a
      solid start on the development of a sustainable maintenance culture, something
      that had not been true of Paraguay historically. The experience suggests that
      performance contracting can be successfully introduced into low capacity
      environments with the proper planning and addressing of constraints. MOPC has
      now developed the capacity and experience to plan and manage this type of
      program and is building towards the use of PBCs for more complex, longer term
      rehabilitation and maintenance contracts (CREMA).
   b. Sectoral governance and transparency programs have the potential to play
      an important role in strengthening of road planning and management. The
      initiatives taken through the IGAP were well founded in terms of addressing
      public perception and civic participation. But they also directly and positively
      impact road management, enhancing the efficiency and effectiveness of
      investments through improvements to monitoring and problem resolution.
      Through the Paraguay experience, a very effective too for monitoring contracts
      was developed. Starting from a quite basic model, the functionality of the tool and
      the general accessibility of information was steadily improved over time. These
      programs should and could be replicable in other countries with however duel
      attention needing to be paid to: ensuring the timetable is not overoptimistic and
      allowing needed time to build capacity as well as environment for improvement;
      and maintaining a healthy balance between completing works and improvements
      in infrastructure with transparency and openness to public participation.




                                            32
c. Attention has to be paid at Project design to ensure the risk assessment is
   truly aligned with the implementation schedule. Both the Bank and the
   implementing agency are subject to pressures from their respective stakeholder
   communities to deliver projects with complex objectives within relatively short
   time frames. However it would be important to recall that implementing a
   challenging institutional change agenda will tend to run into obstacles along the
   way. It would be advisable to assess with due care and attention all dimensions of
   the change process to identify and try to mitigate specific risk factors. There will
   also be the need to give reasonable accommodation to the impact of political and
   administrative change along the way. This Project provides examples of the risks
   to design and implementation of complex and/or new project initiatives in a low
   capacity environment. These risks include the learning process on environment
   and social mitigation measures which can be much longer than estimated upfront.
d. In project design, specific care and attention needs to be given to defining
   clear, measurable and achievable indicators. It is necessary, but not sufficient,
   that a project adequately and clearly address national and sectoral goals. For
   effective project monitoring however, it is critical that the results framework
   include clear, measurable indicators with baseline and target values. In this
   instance there were deficiencies in the design and coverage of these indicators,
   which could and should have been addressed much earlier than they were
   eventually, that is at the time of the January 2014 restructuring. It is important
   that the indicators fully reflect all of the most important benefits of the project, as
   otherwise there is a risk that the evaluation of results may miss certain key
   factors. .
e. Where maintenance funding is such a critical consideration for
   sustainability, the project needs to ensure attention is properly paid. The
   project in this case rightly looked at measures for improving efficiency and
   effectiveness of sector institutions to improve maintenance performance as well as
   for the aforementioned introduction of PBCs. However it was clear that these
   efforts could only go so far when there was persistent underfunding of
   maintenance needs by government, a situation only marginally mitigated by direct
   funding of users. This suggests that more attention has to be given to this factor in
   project monitoring and that consideration ought to be given in project design to
   the inclusion of indicators and targets to track these critical financing flows.
f. Financing of contracts through joint contributions of the Government and
   the Bank should be avoided. One of a number of factors in play in regard to
   delays in project implementation in this case was the financing in the ratio of
   70:30 from the IBRD Loan and from Government. This exacerbated delays in
   contract payments when the two sources of funding could not be aligned. There
   is no overarching reason to maintain this situation given that all the funds are
   ultimately the Government’s. It would have been better to finance contracts
   either 100% by the IBRD loan or 100% by other sources. In certain
   contemporaneous Bank funded projects, the initial co-financing arrangements
   have been modified through a project restructuring, which would have been an
   avenue to explore in this case.

At appraisal, the MOPC performed the economic analysis of the civil works project
components: (i) performance-based maintenance contracts; (ii) improvement of unpaved
roads; and (iii) improvement of bridges. The measured project benefits were savings in
vehicle operating costs and travel time costs and maintenance costs as a result of
improved ride quality and travel times. For paved roads, net benefits were evaluated
using the Highway Development and Management Model (HDM-4) Version 1.3, which
simulates life cycle conditions and costs and provides economic decision criteria for
multiple road design and maintenance alternatives. For unpaved roads, net benefits were
evaluated using the Roads Economic Decision Model (RED) Version 3.2, which adopts
the same economic analysis principles and road user costs as HDM-4, being tailored for
the economic evaluation of unpaved roads.

Ex Post Economic Analysis

The actual annual traffic growth over the evaluation was estimated based on the observed
annual traffic growth data from 2011 to 2014 collected on sixteen toll stations and the GDP
increase observed over the project evaluation period. The traffic on the toll stations
increased at 8.1% per year from 2011 to 2014 and the GDP at constant prices increased at

estimated that the actual traffic growth rate from 2007 to 2015 was 7.0% percent per year,
which is almost three times the annual traffic growth rate estimated at appraisal (2.5% per
year).

INTRODUCCIÓN


Este documento es el informe de cierre del Proyecto de Rehabilitación y Mejoramiento de
la Red Vial del Paraguay “Ñamoporá Ñanderapé”, proyecto que fue financiado con
recursos de crédito de la banca multilateral, Banco Mundial-BIRF-PA-7406, y recursos del
presupuesto nacional, ejecutado en el marco del Convenio suscrito entre el Banco Mundial
y la República de Paraguay.
Los insumos fundamentales de este documento han sido los documentos que reposan en
los archivos digitales y físicos del Proyecto dentro de las instalaciones del MOPC,
información relevante que sirvió para compilar, resumir y analizar desde la concepción y
puesta en marcha hasta el cierre y posterior evaluación del Proyecto.
El informe recoge el cierre de los componentes desarrollados durante la vida del Proyecto
7406 PA, ejecutados por el Ministerio de Obras Públicas y Comunicaciones, a través del
Viceministerio de Obras Públicas y las Direcciones intervinientes como la Dirección de
Vialidad y la Dirección de Planificación Vial, la Unidad Ejecutora GMANS creada a través
del Préstamo y la Unidad de Preparación y Gestión de Proyectos (UPGP) unidad de gestión,
supervisión y administradora del Préstamo 7406 PA.
Así mismo, se hace una recopilación y actualización de la evaluación de la ejecución del
Proyecto, teniendo en cuenta el cumplimiento de los objetivos planteados, cumplimiento
de los compromisos asumidos, cumplimiento de los compromisos del Banco Mundial, las
lecciones aprendidas y las recomendaciones realizadas, la estrategia de sostenibilidad del
Proyecto, el resumen de los archivos físicos y digitales del Proyecto, el cierre
administrativo y financiero del Proyecto parcial a setiembre 2.016, y la actualización de las
fichas de seguimiento y ejecución de proyectos.

ANTECEDENTES

El Gobierno de la República del Paraguay y el Banco Mundial han firmado el Convenio
de Préstamo 7406-PA, para la elaboración del Programa de Mejoramiento, Gestión y
Mantenimiento de la Red Vial del Paraguay. La administración y coordinación del
Programa es responsabilidad del Ministerio de Obras Públicas y Comunicaciones (MOPC)
del Paraguay, a través de la Unidad de Preparación y Gestión de Proyecto (UPGP).
El objetivo general del Programa es el de establecer una Estrategia de Gestión para la Red
Vial nacional que apunte al uso racional de los recursos públicos destinados al
mejoramiento y mantenimiento de las carreteras.
Los objetivos específicos del Programa son: a) Capacitar y brindar las herramientas
necesarias al MOPC a fin de que mejoren la planificación de la red vial;

b) Prevenir el deterioro y mejorar el nivel de servicio a los usuarios de caminos que forman
parte de las carreteras de integración internacional y regional que componen la red nacional
pavimentada, (Rutas 1, 2, 3, 4, 7 y 8);




                                             50
 c) Mejorar la accesibilidad de las comunidades rurales más pobres a la red nacional
pavimentada en tres departamentos del país para una efectiva integración económica y
social, a través del mejoramiento y mantenimiento de caminos nacionales y
departamentales no pavimentados, beneficiando a zonas con mayores necesidades de
ocupación y empleo.

El Programa tiene por fin contribuir al crecimiento económico y la reducción de la pobreza
del país, mejorando las condiciones de la infraestructura vial, el acceso rural, y el impacto
del presupuesto público; promoviendo la transparencia a través del uso de mecanismos
basados en el desempeño, disponibles para el conocimiento de todos los actores
involucrados, y mecanismos participativos.

El Proyecto se conforma de tres componentes:
     Fortalecimiento Institucional, con el objetivo de desarrollar las capacidades y
       herramientas institucionales del MOPC para una planificación estratégica y gestión
       vial integrada y programática de la red vial;

       Red Vial Nacional Pavimentada, con el objetivo de prevenir el deterioro y
        mejorar el nivel de servicios a los usuarios de los corredores viales de integración
        internacional y regional que componen la red vial fundamental del Paraguay;

       Caminos Nacionales y Departamentales no Pavimentados, con el objetivo de
        mejorar el acceso desde zonas rurales (y particularmente desde las comunidades
        rurales más pobres y marginales) a la Red Vial Nacional pavimentada, para
        fomentar una efectiva integración económica y social del país.

El objetivo principal del Proyecto de establecer una Estrategia de Gestión para la Red Vial
nacional se conseguirá apuntando al uso racional de los recursos públicos destinados al
mejoramiento y mantenimiento de las carreteras y buscando (i) transparencia, mediante la
planificación y programación de todas las intervenciones basadas en criterios objetivos y
cuantificables; (ii) mejorar el desempeño; mediante la identificación de metas para cada
intervención, que serán monitoreadas y cuyo cumplimiento será la indicación del éxito de
la misma, (iii) la divulgación; mediante la participación, publicación, y presentación de las
acciones programadas a los actores y a los beneficiarios involucrados, así como la
publicación y presentación de los resultados obtenidos.

Este acontecer se ve reflejado en la ejecución del Préstamo en los dos primeros años de
implementación.

En una Etapa que denominaremos intermedia; se procede a la contratación de consultores
de planta a fin de ayudar en la implementación y mejorar la ejecución, los consultores
contratados fueron técnicos de las diferentes aéreas a ejecutarse, de esta forma se logra un
fortalecimiento institucional de la UPGP y se forma un grupo de trabajo conscientes del
compromiso contraído y con la ayuda que significó las capacitaciones realizadas por el
Banco Mundial, se fueron dando más celeridad a las gestiones de los documentos.




                                             56
En cuanto a los procedimientos internos al MOPC, las gestiones para las licitaciones
tuvieron mucho retraso, debido principalmente a que los funcionarios del mismo conocen
los procesos la Ley 2051, que regula las contrataciones nacionales; no así las Normas de
adquisiciones de los organismos financieros internacionales como el BIRF.

En la Etapa final del Préstamo las capacidades adquiridas del personal consultor y
funcionariado y la comunicación constante con el Banco Mundial para los procedimientos
se fueron agilizando llegando inclusive a que las Licitaciones fueran publicadas con un
ahorro del 50 % de tiempo invertido que al comenzar el Préstamo.

Actualmente se observan como aspectos positivos:
   I.  Pro actividad y mejoras en gestión y control de la ejecución;
  II.  aumentos de la eficacia en la ejecución de los proyectos.
 III.  Seguimiento y evaluación, en constante proceso de mejora
 IV. Gestión financiera adecuada
  V. Significativos progresos en las relaciones con otras instituciones nacionales y
       gobernaciones



LECCIONES APRENDIDAS Y RECOMENDACIONES

   1. Respecto a los Contratos GMANS:

 El promedio de longitud de los GMASN interurbanos diseñados (1 a 6) ha sido de 317
km / contrato. Lo aconsejable es tener mallas más acotadas, desde ya dependiendo de las
condiciones geográficas y características de las mismas, en el entorno de los 150 km de
longitud promedio de la Malla. El GMANS urbano tuvo una longitud de 108 km, y resulta
lógico una longitud menor en centros urbanos.

Dependiendo de las características geográficas el dimensionamiento podría estar en el
entorno entre los 100 km y 200 km aproximadamente, con un promedio de 150 km. La
inversión inicial de obras de puesta a punto también incide en la decisión de la longitud de
las mallas de caminos que conformen una unidad económica rentable para el sector
privado.

 Los tramos que conforman la malla del contrato GMANS deben estar agrupados por
tramos homogéneos bien definidos, agrupados por rangos de tránsito, estructura del
pavimento existente y condición de estado.

Para ello es necesario contar con un Informe de Investigación de la zona de obras que
realice el MOPC para definir el denominado Proyecto Básico o Referencial de las OPP
mínimas: i) relevamiento de estado, ii) medición de IRI, iii) deflexiones, iv) espesores, v)
calicatas y clasificación de suelos, vi) estudios de tránsitos disponibles, vii) inventario
físico para que el oferente evalúe lo faltante según normativa vigente (ejemplo señalización
vertical, estado de alcantarillas, etc.).


                                            57
 Para el umbral de IRI a exigir al final del contrato, deben hacerse una proyección de
IRI correlacionado con modelos de deterioro como AASHTO, o bien utilizar el HDM-4
verificando condiciones de calibración.

 Se requiere que el PBC defina expresamente cuales son las Obras de Puesta a Punto,
estas obras debe indicarse que son mínimas y que el contratista deberá evaluar a su riesgo
las obras necesarias para poder cumplir con los umbrales establecidos, bajo el concepto de
suma alzada y no pensar en el concepto de unidad de medida. Los pliegos establecen obras
obligatorias y se debería adaptar al concepto de obra mínima necesaria para cumplir el
estándar. Esta definición de las OPP debe incluir el riesgo de la carga circulante.

 Es importante definir un Precio Base acorde con un Proyecto Base bien estudiado con
mediciones de la red, ya que es una disposición a pagar el Estado y una referencia para la
definición posterior del monto del contrato. Para ello se requiere de un archivo estadístico,
datos de inventario y necesidad de fortalecer el desarrollo de las capacidades institucionales
del MOPC.

 Los estándares a exigirse en el DDL deben estar particularizados para cada uno de los
tramos homogéneos que componen la malla de rutas del proyecto GMANS. Para ello debe
definirse estándares de exigencias específicos para cada tramo homogéneo. También se
recomienda que los tramos homogéneos sean de una longitud aceptable para el manejo del
contrato (10 a 20 km, dependiendo de cada malla y por supuesto de las características del
tramo).

 Definir un plazo de al menos 60 días hasta 90 días, para la presentación del Diseño
Ejecutivo por parte de la Contratista, que incluya todas las mediciones técnicas e informes
de ingeniería para cumplir con los estándares exigidos, y que definan las obras de puesta a
punto, como resultado de las evaluaciones estructurales y no solo superficiales. Debe
preverse un plazo en función de la longitud y complejidad del tramo.

 Ante el planteo de los Contratistas de la posibilidad de reubicar por tramos las
cantidades mínimas según la necesidad, esto debe salvarse y definirse a la aprobación del
Diseño Ejecutivo, según las justificaciones técnicas que presente el Contratista, con las
mediciones e informes de ingeniería correspondientes.

 Resulta necesario la participación de la Fiscalización en el Diseño Ejecutivo, por
desfasaje en el inicio de sus Servicios respecto a la Obra. En este sentido la Fiscalizadora
debería estar contratada durante el proceso de adjudicación de la obra o bien, como se
propone en el punto de Fiscalizadoras, contratarle proyecto básico y fiscalización.

 Se recomienda definir los plazos en función de la longitud y complejidad de la obra,
éstos deberían contarse a partir de la fecha de inicio de las obras de puesta a punto, es decir
a los 10 o 15 días de aprobado el Diseño Ejecutivo, y el plazo de considerar los tiempos de
movilización + replanteo + tiempo necesario para ejecutar las obras de puesta a punto.
Debe emitirse la orden de inicio una vez aprobado el Diseño Ejecutivo presentado por la


                                              58
Contratista y estudiado por la fiscalización. A su vez, iniciada las obras de puesta a punto,
regirán los plazos para la puesta a punto, en una primera etapa se deben lograr los
estándares básicos y en la segunda que se produce a continuación de la etapa 1 y finaliza a
la terminación del plazo de las obras de puesta a punto.

 Se debería definir una metodología más precisa para implementación de las multas y
bonificaciones, ya que pueden quedar a criterio de la fiscalizadora. Asimismo la severidad
de la penalidad y la extensión del incumplimiento. Además, se recomienda aplicar
penalidades ante un nivel de servicio determinado (ejemplo 95%) y no aplicar
bonificaciones, ya que conceptualmente el contratista tiene que cumplir ese umbral mínimo
y no debería recibir bonificación por un umbral que debe cumplir si lo supera.

 Otras Intervenciones Obligatorias: En el DDL podrían incorporarse otras actividades
tales como: control de cargas, inventario vial, relevamiento de estado, medición de
rugosidades, mediciones de tránsito.

   4. Respecto al Mejoramiento de Caminos No Pavimentados:

Este proyecto previó en un principio el mejoramiento de 960 km de Caminos No
Pavimentados. En el 2008 se solicitó la inclusión de la Ruta IV (San Ignacio – Pilar) en los
contratos GMANS. Esto requirió la asignación de importantes recursos del Préstamo, lo
cual obligó a disminuir los Caminos No Pavimentados a ser mejorados dentro del Proyecto.



                                             59
Los tramos que finalmente fueron ejecutados suman 140 km de longitud y se detallan a
continuación:

      San Pedro:
          o Lote 1:Chore - Colonia Cocuerá - San Pablo
          o Lote 2: Chore - Jhugua Rey
          o Lote 3: Jhugua Rey - Gral. Aquino

      Caaguazú: Colonias Unidas - Yjhovy Central - Raúl A. Oviedo - J. Eulogio
       Estigarribia

      Caazapá: Caazapá – Boquerón

Respecto a los tramos en el Departamento de San Pedro, los Lote 2 y 3 se ejecutaron sin
mayores problemas, en el plazo establecido en el contrato. Respecto al Lote 1; se generaron
disturbios por parte de los pobladores, quienes se encontraron divididos en 2 grupos en la
zona de Colonia Cocuerá: uno que pedía que el mejoramiento pase por la zona conocida
como Tapé Tuja, y otro que pedía que pase por el cementerio. Amenazaron con no permitir
que los equipo trabajen si no se cumplía con sus solicitudes, incluso con quemar los
mismos. Se solicitó al Banco Mundial la No Objeción para ampliar el contrato y mejorar
ambos tramos, para evitar el problema social generado, y se procedió a la elaboración del
diseño del tramo faltante (4 km aprox.), posteriormente se solicitó la cotización de los
adicionales generados y se procedió a ejecutar dichos adicionales. Todo este proceso
generó también prórrogas en los plazos.

Lección aprendida: los problemas sociales pueden ocasionar problemas graves en las Obras
como retrasos, perjuicios a los equipos del contratista, sobrecostos, tanto para el contratista
como para la fiscalización.

Recomendación: una mejor evaluación de los grupos sociales, y socialización del proyecto
en etapas previas al llamado.

Caaguazú: en el diseño no se previeron las cañerías de la red de agua. Esto generó atrasos
en los inicios de la Obra. Se solicitó el levantamiento de red existente y el re diseño de la
nueva red. Esto generó prorroga de plazos y adicionales.

Recomendación: Minimizar los imprevistos, incluyendo el relevamiento de las redes
subterráneas existentes en los pliegos de Diseño.

Caazapá: Esta obra se encuentra en zonas de prolongadas inundaciones, además el plano
de fundación de los pilotes para puentes resultó estar en un estrato más profundo de lo
previsto. Esto generó prórrogas muy importantes, con un plazo final mayor al triple del
plazo inicial.

Recomendación: Incluir verificación de los estudios de suelo del periodo de diseño entre
las actividades del Contratista, a fin de verificar la profundidad de las fundaciones.
Además analizar mejor las zonas inundables, y prever plazos más realistas según la zona.
